I started making pop and applied music for the mainland Chinese market in 2017, mostly as a composer, arranger and producer, for clients in film, TV and games as well as ordinary pop listeners. In 2021 I joined Professor Zhiyao Duan's lab at the University of Rochester, where I started with busy work and slowly moved toward deep learning; I now lead research and labs at Smule, working with a lean team to figure out which models to train and how to train them. Since 2025 I've put less and less of my energy into music production, and these days I mostly write demos for friends and rarely do the arranging myself or take a song through the full production process.

I have a lot going on now, so the time I can spend focused on one song is probably under an hour. The first half hour goes to the melody, lyrics, chords and a rough groove. I'm used to writing at the keyboard, and I sketch these out quickly with a piano or guitar sound. I'm much faster than a model at this step, and making the demo also makes it clearer to me what I want. After that, I take ten minutes or so to lay out the other instruments I want and roughly where they should go. I usually use Logic Pro X, because I know it well and its stock instruments are reasonably good.

Then I turn to a music generation model like Suno and, mostly by editing the text description, have it generate the overall result I'm after. Here the model mainly helps me swap instruments, try different instrumentations, and make a demo I didn't sing very carefully sound better; where I haven't fully worked out what I want myself, I use it to try a few more versions until I find it. I usually pick a few versions, download all the stems, edit and remix them in Logic, and hand that in.

Unfortunately, what these models produce still can't be handed in as a finished arrangement and production; compared with where language models and video models are today, the gap is fairly large. In this post I want to make a guess: the gap exists mainly because music generation models need finer-grained text descriptions. To write such descriptions, a model first has to understand the music it hears, and the data needed to train that understanding is something we don't have yet.

## Models keep getting bigger and the metrics keep going up, but they don't sound better

I often hear two views in the industry: one says the capabilities of music generation models scale easily, the other says they don't.

If the goal is only to fit the pretraining data better, or to fit some set of human preferences better, there's no reason it shouldn't scale, and by and large it does. YuE compared three sizes, 0.5B, 2B and 7B, and in human evaluation, the larger the model and the training budget, the more its musicality was preferred [@yue]. MusicGen reported three sizes, 300M, 1.5B and 3.3B: perplexity fell from 56.1 to 48.4 to 46.1 and FAD from 0.96 to 0.86 to 0.82, both improving steadily [@musicgen]. Mikey Shulman, the CEO of Suno, also said in a 2024 interview that their models scale much like text models do, and that "bigger is usually better" [@shulman2024].

But for the same MusicGen models, the overall quality rated by humans (OVL) is 78.3, 81.9 and 79.2, and it stops rising at 1.5B. The authors say so themselves: subjective quality no longer improves past 1.5B, and the 3.3B model only seems to follow the text prompt more closely [@musicgen]. There is a similar observation in speech: Cuervo and Marxer found that the linguistic ability of speech-only language models scales up to three orders of magnitude more slowly than that of text models [@cuervo2024]. Audio simply asks more of the data and the supervision signal.

I think both views are right. A bigger model does fit better, but judging from the human ratings above, it doesn't necessarily sound better. Below are the three problems I run into most often in day-to-day use. I think they affect the usability of these models to a large degree, and that the solution behind all three is the same: finer-grained text descriptions.

### The model only gets the gist of my text instructions

As I said at the start, I mostly get the result I want out of the model by editing the text description. The model should be able to understand the instruction I give it and use it to narrow the search space of its outputs; a good instruction is one that pins the model down. In image and video, we can require the output to satisfy certain pixel-level constraints, such as a luminance map, a depth map, normals derived from an untextured 3D model, or the identity of a particular character. If I paste in a depth map, the model follows that depth map; if I ask for a fixed character, the model keeps that character.

In music generation, though, we often can't get control that direct. Some controls are direct, like melody, lyrics and singer identity; some are more or less direct, like instruments and groove; and some are very vague and coarse, like style or a certain emotional feel. To some extent, a creator's skill lies in turning vague controls into direct ones, step by step.

Research and products are both making control more concrete, bit by bit. Instruct-MusicGen lets the model add, remove or extract a single stem when instructed [@instructmusicgen]; Suno Studio can generate stems and export them as audio or MIDI [@sunostudio]. But as soon as the instruction gets vague, the results fall off quickly. RIME has multimodal LLM agents do mixing and post-production from instructions: when the instruction states the effects and parameters directly, the edit similarity is 0.718 for GPT-Audio-Mini and 0.687 for Gemma 3n; when it only describes the overall feel, the two drop to 0.519 and 0.528 respectively [@rime].

Without this kind of fine-grained, direct control, I can't really control what a music generation model outputs.

### Instruments change while they're playing

The second problem is that I often hear instruments change on their own in the generated result. The most typical case is a brass section blending into a string section that plays a similar role, with instruments that should stay on one melodic line jumping around; the vocal comes and goes, or suddenly turns into another instrument. The drum sound and the soundstage of the song often jump as well. Subtler jumps happen in almost every generation: I hear the size of the guitar, the recording conditions and the texture of the strings changing constantly. If I ask for a single brass instrument, what I get is often a continuous "spectrum of brass instruments" that slides from trumpet all the way to tuba, with some chance of sliding on to cello, and the cello may well slide on to double bass.

Papers report similar things. The SongGen authors note that when the mix is used directly as the training target, the model tends to prioritize the more predictable accompaniment and often neglects the vocal features [@songgen]. A challenge paper trained on only 457 hours of data found that distinctive instruments such as saxophone, harmonica and vibraphone are often rendered as generic timbres [@koh2026].

I suspect this has to do with how the model represents audio. A very common practice in the industry now is to first compress audio into more "semantic" discrete tokens, which are easier for a language model to model. The approach comes from speech research: MusicLM largely keeps the structure of AudioLM, using w2v-BERT to produce semantic tokens and SoundStream to produce acoustic tokens [@musiclm].

In music, the line between semantic and acoustic is not that clear, but people still train this way, and each group makes its own assumption about what counts as semantic. MERT uses an RVQ-VAE as the acoustic teacher and adds a CQT-based musical teacher [@mert]; MuQ predicts tokens produced by Mel-RVQ [@muq]; MuCodec first does masked language modeling, then adds mel-spectrogram and CQT reconstruction along with a lyric transcription loss made up of CTC and RNN-T [@mucodec]. The MuSeC authors put it more bluntly: these semantics-guided schemes were designed for speech and don't fit music well [@musec].

But what exactly is "semantics" in music? Nobody seems to have ever defined it clearly. If we train with masked prediction, we are in effect saying that the target that gets masked out for the model to predict is the semantics; unfortunately, we often don't know whether that target contains all the semantic information it should. If we instead ask the tokenizer to learn to output text descriptions and lyrics from audio, then the boundary of the semantics is drawn by those descriptions and lyrics.

My guess is that this is related to the jumps I hear. If the line itself is not drawn clearly, things that are "semantically" close, such as trumpet and tuba or cello and double bass, may be treated by the model as roughly the same thing and lumped together.

I think one reason this approach is enough for speech but not for music is that information is structured differently in the two. Speech mostly has a single speaker whose identity is stable, and the information basically changes along a single axis, time. Music has both a horizontal and a vertical dimension: vertically, several parts are stacked at the same moment, with sources that overlap heavily and are much harder to pull apart than in speech [@mousavi2025]; horizontally, the function of each part keeps changing over time.

### Everything it generates sounds about the same

The third problem is mode collapse, which I can observe very easily. However I change the prompt, what comes out is about the same; the model is too opinionated. Put simply, RL has been overdone. The odd thing is that it also looks as if RL hasn't been done enough: control is still poor, yet the output is already very homogeneous, somewhat like the collapse we saw in the GAN era.

A paper from August 2026 happened to measure exactly this [@slendebroek2026]. The authors analyzed the output of Suno and Lyria 3 across four genres with 72 MIR features and found that Lyria 3 has lower diversity within a genre, while Suno flattens the differences between genres. Neither system follows the prompt faithfully, and an ordinary classifier can separate AI tracks from human tracks almost perfectly.

There is already a fair amount of evidence that RLHF reduces diversity in music. SMART tuned a symbolic music generation system using Audiobox Aesthetics as the reward and found that over-optimization sharply reduces output diversity; after optimization, the outputs often score higher on this reward than real performances do, which, as the authors themselves say, only shows the limitation of using this reward as a proxy metric [@smart]. DRAGON found that optimizing per-song FAD worsens Vendi diversity by 17.7% on average [@dragon]. On language models, Kirk et al. likewise found that RLHF output is markedly less diverse than SFT output [@kirk2023].

Beyond RL, the classifier-free guidance commonly used at inference also pushes diversity down: on a MusicLM-based model, raising the guidance scale from 1 to 7 drops the diversity score from 0.5 to 0.3 [@cideron2024cfg].

Part of RLHF's diversity problem comes from the mode-seeking behavior of RL itself. GX-Chen et al. go a step further and point out that under common settings (weak regularization, or all correct answers receiving the same reward), the optimal target distribution of KL-regularized RL is often unimodal to begin with, so the collapse of diversity is in effect written into the objective [@gxchen2025].

<details>
<summary>Derivation: why is the optimal distribution unimodal?</summary>

**Setup**: Let $\pi$ be the model being trained, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ the reference model (usually the model before RL starts), $R(y)$ the reward of an output $y$, and $\beta > 0$ the regularization strength. KL-regularized RL (here meaning the most common form, reverse-KL regularization) maximizes

$$
J_\beta(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\big[R(y)\big] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big). \tag{1}
$$

**Optimal solution**: Expanding the KL term by its definition and absorbing the reward into the logarithm,

$$
\begin{aligned}
J_\beta(\pi)
&= \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\Big[R(y) - \beta \log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}\Big] \\
&= -\beta\, \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\Big[\log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\, e^{R(y)/\beta}}\Big] \\
&= -\beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi \,\|\, G_\beta\big) + \beta \log Z_\beta ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
\begin{aligned}
G_\beta(y) &= \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\, e^{R(y)/\beta}}{Z_\beta}, \\
Z_\beta &= \sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y')\, e^{R(y')/\beta}.
\end{aligned} \tag{2}
$$

$Z_\beta$ does not depend on $\pi$, and the KL divergence is non-negative and zero only when the two distributions are identical, so (1) has exactly one optimal solution: $\pi^* = G_\beta$. In other words, even with unlimited compute and perfect optimization, RL will only ever arrive at $G_\beta$. It is the "target distribution" of this objective.

**Probability ratio**: Take any two outputs $y_1$ and $y_2$. The normalizing constant $Z_\beta$ cancels in the ratio, so

$$
\log \frac{G_\beta(y_1)}{G_\beta(y_2)} = \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2)} + \frac{R(y_1) - R(y_2)}{\beta}. \tag{3}
$$

The first term on the right is the reference model's existing preference, and the second is the reward difference divided by $\beta$. Which of the two outputs gets more probability in the target distribution is determined entirely by these two terms.

**Case one**: all correct answers have the same reward. This is the standard setup for verifiable tasks, with 1 for a correct answer and 0 for a wrong one. For any two correct answers, the second term of (3) is zero, so

$$
\frac{G_\beta(y_1)}{G_\beta(y_2)} = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2)},
$$

and this ratio does not depend on $\beta$. RL only moves probability from wrong answers to correct ones, while the relative proportions among the correct answers are carried over from the reference model unchanged: whichever kind of answer the reference model favored, the target distribution favors to the same degree, and lowering $\beta$ doesn't help. As long as the reference model already assigns these answers very different probabilities, the target distribution is concentrated on one of them in the same way. Replacing the regularizer with forward KL doesn't change this conclusion.

**Case two**: the rewards differ and the regularization is weak. Consider first two outputs with the same reference probability, for which (3) becomes

$$
\frac{G_\beta(y_1)}{G_\beta(y_2)} = \exp\!\Big(\frac{R(y_1) - R(y_2)}{\beta}\Big).
$$

A linear gap in reward becomes an exponential gap in probability. The paper's example: with rewards that differ by only 0.1 and $\beta$ set to the commonly used $10^{-3}$, the ratio is $e^{100}$, a factor of more than $10^{43}$. The conclusion is the same when the reference probabilities differ: the first term of (3) is fixed while the second keeps growing as $\beta$ shrinks, so for small enough $\beta$, the output with the slightly higher reward always ends up with the overwhelming majority.

**Condition for multimodality**: For two outputs to have the same probability in the target distribution, the right-hand side of (3) must be zero, that is,

$$
R(y_2) - R(y_1) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2)}. \tag{4}
$$

The reward difference has to exactly cancel the difference in log probability under the reference model. $\beta$ is only a knob here: turned up, it favors the outputs the reference model likes; turned down, it favors the outputs with higher reward. As long as the two outputs have different rewards, there is at most one $\beta$ that makes them exactly even. Common settings generally don't satisfy this condition, so most of the probability in the target distribution sits on one mode rather than being shared among all the high-quality outputs. The paper calls the latter case multimodal, and the former is what I call unimodal here. This collapse isn't caused by insufficient exploration or poor optimization: the better the optimization, the closer the model gets to $G_\beta$, and the more complete the collapse.

</details>

The other part comes from the reward model: we have no way to inspect what it has actually learned or what it is rewarding. So the most common practice is to watch the RL process, pull out some checkpoints and listen to them ourselves. Listening this way, it's hard to hear a lack of diversity, and we can only hear some problems with how the rewards are set up; once a problem is found, the usual practice is to add another reward, and then another.

Rewards patched together like this can easily affect generalization. The DiffRhythm 2 authors write that as the number of preferences grows, model merging often noticeably lowers the average performance across them [@diffrhythm2]. TuneJury found that while the reward goes up, MAD, which measures distributional fidelity, gets worse: with expert iteration at a learning rate of 1e-5, the reward rose by 0.416 and MAD worsened by 0.669, which the authors call a classic signature of reward exploitation [@tunejury].

More fundamentally, everyone's taste in music is different. MusicRL collected 300,000 pairwise user preferences and found that text adherence and audio quality account for only part of them, and that fine-tuning with the quality reward alone is also prone to over-optimization [@musicrl]. CMI-RewardBench re-annotated 500 test samples: Krippendorff's α between annotators was only around 0.50, and Gemini-2.5-Pro's agreement with human preferences also struggled to exceed 80% [@cmirewardbench].

The aesthetic data and metrics we have all record some kind of consensus taste. SongEval had 16 professional annotators with music backgrounds rate 2,399 full songs on 5 dimensions [@songeval]; Audiobox Aesthetics breaks aesthetics down into four dimensions: production quality, production complexity, content enjoyment and content usefulness [@audioboxaesthetics]. Xiao et al. also show theoretically that KL-regularized RLHF can, in extreme cases, exhibit preference collapse, in which minority preferences are almost entirely disregarded [@xiao2024].

Even in text, where data is plentiful, investment is heavy and research resources are ample, this problem hasn't been fully solved. People have simply broken the reward into finer and more objective pieces, and put most of their training effort elsewhere.

## How does vision do it?

As I said at the start, music generation models are well behind video models today, so I want to look first at how the vision side does it. For some time now, the vast majority of vision research teams have put most of their effort into one thing: finer-grained text descriptions.

At training time, they use a model to rewrite the descriptions of the training data, and this ability to write descriptions can borrow directly from the prior of text models. DALL·E 3 trained a dedicated captioner to write descriptive synthetic captions and ended up training on 95% synthetic captions plus 5% original ones; the paper also notes that GPT-4 can "upsample" any short caption into a highly detailed description [@dalle3]. PixArt-α relabeled LAION images with LLaVA, which raised the number of nouns per caption from 6.4 to 20.9 and the share of valid nouns from 8.5% to 13.3%; for SAM images labeled with LLaVA, the figures are 29.3 and 18.6% [@pixartalpha]. SD3 replaced half of its training captions with ones generated by CogVLM, and its overall GenEval score rose from 43.27 to 49.78, with position going from 6.50 to 18.00 and color attribution from 11.75 to 24.75 [@sd3].

The same goes for video. CogVideoX first has CogVLM write a dense caption for every frame, then has GPT-4 summarize them into a caption for the whole video, and finally uses the output of this pipeline to train an end-to-end video captioner [@cogvideox]. ShareGPT4Video has GPT-4V describe, frame by frame, what changes between adjacent frames; it labeled 40K videos this way, trained ShareCaptioner-Video on them, and then used that model to label 4.8M video clips [@sharegpt4video]. Newer models such as SAM 3 can, given a concept, detect and segment every matching object in each frame of a video and track it throughout [@sam3]; this information is also often used to synthesize more detailed captions.

At inference time, they use a text model to expand the user's short prompt into a detailed description. The Sora technical report states this clearly: it applies DALL·E 3's re-captioning to video and uses GPT to expand short user prompts into long captions, which are then sent to the video model [@sora]. Movie Gen uses LLaMA3 70B as a teacher to distill an 8B prompt rewriter [@moviegen]; HunyuanVideo likewise devotes a section to prompt rewriting [@hunyuanvideo]. Prompt Expansion, for its part, found that sampling several expanded versions of the same prompt yields images that are more diverse and also better looking [@promptexpansion].

Of course, denser captions are not always better. In controlled experiments, Brack et al. found that long, dense synthetic captions improve text alignment but can come at the cost of aesthetics and sample diversity; randomizing caption length during training eases this trade-off [@brack2025].

So to a large extent, control over video generation models has become finer and more specific, and has come to depend more and more on the intelligence and understanding of language models.

Vision research teams have also been very active in using synthetic data to keep widening the range that descriptions can cover, a point I'll come back to when discussing music. Playing for Data rendered 25K images with GTA V, and game data plus one third of CamVid slightly outperformed a model trained on the full CamVid [@playingfordata]. The teacher in Depth Anything V2 is trained entirely on synthetic images [@depthanythingv2]. CoSyn has an LLM write code to render 400K images and 2.7M rows of instruction data, and its average performance on text-rich benchmarks exceeds that of GPT-4V and Gemini 1.5 Flash [@cosyn].

## Music is doing fine-grained descriptions too, but they're not fine enough

Using a model to label training data is already routine in music generation as well. LeVo used Qwen2-Audio to give about 2 million songs open-vocabulary tags [@levo]. ACE-Step 1.5 first labeled 5 million samples with Gemini 2.5 Pro as a golden set, then fine-tuned Qwen2.5-Omni into a dedicated captioner, refined it further with a reward model and RL, and finally labeled the full corpus of 27 million samples [@acestep15]. Captioners themselves are also getting stronger: Music Flamingo's MF-Skills data covers harmony, structure, timbre, lyrics and cultural context [@musicflamingo], and Qwen has released Qwen3-Omni Captioner as a separate model [@qwen3omnicaptioner].

There is work on the time dimension too. FUTGA points out that existing music captioning methods can mostly only write a brief overall description of a short clip, and so built fine-grained captions with temporal information [@futga]. SegTune uses Audio Flamingo 3 to write a separate caption for each section, then lays each section's prompt over the corresponding stretch of time as a condition for generation [@segtune].

But these understanding models still fall short in capability. Ideally, a fine-grained description of a piece of music should be detailed enough that the text, written out in full, maps back to one complete piece of audio, just as a sufficiently detailed caption in video maps back to one video. Today's models are still quite far from this standard. MusTBench ran its tests in 2026 and found that existing audio language models generally struggle even with precise temporal grounding, especially with judging when a sound source ends. Music Flamingo's average score on temporal sound grounding (TSG) is only 38.5, and only 24.0 for hitting the end point within 3 seconds [@mustbench].

Since video understanding can already do this, we can infer that the shortfall in capability is mainly a shortfall in data. Fine-grained understanding data really is too expensive. MusicCaps has only 5.5k entries, all handwritten by professional musicians [@musiclm]; and it was precisely because manual collection is "costly and time-consuming" that the LP-MusicCaps authors turned to generating pseudo captions from tags with an LLM [@lpmusiccaps].

## What if we suddenly had fine-grained understanding?

Suppose a perfect dataset of fine-grained descriptions suddenly fell from the sky, with tens of millions of audio clips, each described in enough detail that the audio could almost be recovered from the text. What would happen? Clearly, music understanding would improve very quickly. These understanding models could be used right away to rewrite the descriptions of the training data for generation models, and the ability of generation models to follow instructions would improve along with them. The three problems I described above would all get better too.

First, the model would no longer get only the gist, and creators could truly control it; it would become a more faithful tool in the creator's hands. If a description can recover the audio almost perfectly, it is in effect a "project file" that we can edit however we like. In audio-to-audio work, this understanding can also be used to decide what to keep, what not to keep, and how to change things.

Second, instruments would no longer slide away so easily, because there would be less semantic ambiguity. Many tokenizers already use text as supervision: MuCodec adds a lyric recognition loss [@mucodec], and CosyVoice inserts vector quantization into the encoder of a speech recognition model to obtain supervised semantic tokens [@cosyvoice]. If a codec can produce fine-grained text descriptions, that at least shows the information has been preserved; fine-grained text supervision is exactly the kind of supervision signal it needs.

Representation learning has long had related results: without any inductive bias on the model and the data, learning disentangled representations without supervision is theoretically impossible [@locatello2019]; and models superimpose more features than they have dimensions onto the same set of dimensions [@superposition]. There is also an existing framework for measuring how well a representation separates information and how well it retains it, which looks at disentanglement, completeness and informativeness separately [@dci].

This benefits the downstream rendering too. Flow matching and diffusion models are actually not very good at handling this kind of ambiguity, and I don't think it should be left to them. The clearer the condition, the easier the step from semantic to acoustic, and the better the controllability. Seed-Music mentions that because lead sheet tokens don't capture acoustic features, they needed to scale up the downstream renderer to reach the same end-to-end performance [@seedmusic].

Third, the outputs would no longer all sound alike. Once the search space is constrained by the caption, diversity can be achieved by changing the text, and the reward no longer has to be entangled with shortcomings in instruction following. Kaleido Diffusion takes this approach for images: it first autoregressively generates discrete latents such as text descriptions and bounding boxes, then conditions the diffusion on them, trading richer conditions for more diverse outputs [@kaleido]. This way we can train only the model's ability to follow instructions, which is easy to verify.

Finally, rewriting, expanding and reasoning over text can all be handed directly to existing LLMs. This lets us draw on the strength of language models: the better they get, the better music generation gets.

## This fine-grained description is really a project file

Stem separation, instrument recognition, key, meter, chords, transcription: these traditional MIR problems all come down to how to get a fine-grained caption. Such a dataset won't fall from the sky, of course, and I think it is most likely to come from DAW project files. These annotations used to be expensive, but the cost is now coming down, because GPT-6 Astra can already control a DAW (Digital Audio Workstation) directly, and can even generate DAW project files outright. In a project, everything is known: which note each track plays at which frame, with what sound, and through what effects. In other words, the labels are frame-level and almost free. Given the pace of the past few years, I believe that through quantization and distillation, intelligence at the same level will only keep getting cheaper.

There is already groundwork for this. Slakh2100 rendered Lakh MIDI with professional sample-based instruments into 2100 multitrack mixes totaling 145 hours [@slakh2100]. WildFX puts REAPER in Docker and runs it in headless mode, with support for VST, VST3, LV2 and CLAP plugins, for blind estimation of mixing graphs and plugin parameters [@wildfx]. Systems that let an agent operate a DAW have begun to appear as well: DAWZY has GPT-5 generate Lua scripts that modify a REAPER project through ReaPy, while using three MCP tools to query state, adjust parameters and generate beats [@dawzy]; the community also has bridges such as AbletonMCP [@abletonmcp].

As in vision, the combination of synthetic and real data works in music too. MuScriptor does multi-instrument transcription: trained on synthetic data alone, its onset F1 is 34.5; fine-tuning on real data brings it to 54.4; adding RL brings it to 60.4. The authors conclude that synthetic pretraining is highly effective, especially when real data is scarce, but that synthetic data alone is not enough [@muscriptor]. In fingerstyle guitar transcription, training on purely procedural data gives an F1 of 66.23, purely real data gives 81.42, and synthetic pretraining followed by real fine-tuning gives 83.49 [@murgul2025]. TAC assembles synthetic mixtures from real audio material to train a timestamped audio captioner and reaches state of the art on TACOS, though the authors also acknowledge that a sim-to-real gap remains [@tac].

We have to be honest about this gap between synthetic and real, which comes mainly from the quality of the virtual instruments. SynthSOD trained separation models on synthetic orchestral data, and none of them separated real recordings well, not even the relatively simple URMP. The authors attribute this mainly to the fact that sample-based instruments cycle through a small number of recorded samples in round-robin fashion, so the actual diversity of the data is quite limited [@synthsod].

Suppose we suddenly had virtual instruments of very high quality; we could then have agents generate synthetic data in bulk. Since we know every detail in the project file, the project itself can yield very fine-grained understanding data. Suppose further that agents can arrange like a real person[^agent]; then we would have all the data we need.

[^agent]: They'll get there. Don't underestimate what agents can do.

With the data, we could first train small classifiers one at a time (stem separation, instrument activity detection, key, meter) and then assemble their outputs into a complete caption. One step beyond that is a transcriber that can transcribe every instrument and recover the complete project directly.

Today we are still a long way from that goal. In the 2025 multi-instrument transcription challenge, only two of the eight teams that submitted valid results beat the MT3 baseline [@amtchallenge2025]; systems like YourMT3+ output notes assigned to instruments, that is, pitch and timing, without timbre, effects or mixing parameters yet [@yourmt3plus].

## Using an open DAW project format as the intermediate representation

Besides being used to build understanding data, I think project files can also serve directly as the condition for a generation model. Quite a few works already guide generation with some kind of intermediate condition. Music ControlNet controls generation with time-varying melody, dynamics and rhythm [@musiccontrolnet]; JASCO combines symbolic conditions such as chords and melody with audio conditions for temporally controllable generation [@jasco]; MuseControlLite supports multiple kinds of control with lightweight conditioners [@musecontrollite]; Seed-Music uses lead sheets as its intermediate representation [@seedmusic].

Going further, much recent work first generates a symbolic intermediate representation and then generates the audio: YuE2 first writes out an ABC score [@yue2], StepAudio 3 Music plans the arrangement in ABC notation (ABC-CoT) [@stepaudio3music], and Qwen-Music plans with melody tokens [@qwenmusic].

I think these are all good intermediate steps, but we can go many steps further. If there were an open DAW project format, it would be a better intermediate representation than MIDI or ABC. MIDI basically only covers notes, and ABC records even less; a project file, by contrast, has tracks, clips, notes and expression, automation, plugins and plugin states, so nearly every decision the musician made is recorded in it. Turning it into text and using that as the condition comes very close to having a musician operate the DAW directly. The generation model is then mainly responsible for rendering: the search space is tightly constrained, the distribution it has to learn is more deterministic, and details like instrument timbre are easier to control.

A format like this in fact already exists. DAWproject, introduced by Bitwig and PreSonus, can record the audio, notes, note expression and automation data on the timeline, with plugin states embedded as preset files [@dawproject]. Plugin states use each plugin's own format, but if every state is converted into a sufficiently detailed description in language, that is no longer a problem.

## A shared goal

Putting all this together, the overall goal of AI music as a research field could be to build, as quickly as possible, a large amount of fine-grained text-music paired data, detailed enough that the audio can almost be recovered from the text; and the most feasible technical route at present is to have LLM-driven agents produce synthetic data at scale.

Much of the work mentioned earlier can be seen in light of this goal. Captioners, temporal grounding and transcription go from audio toward description; Slakh2100 and DAW-operating agents go from project toward audio; lead sheets and ABC can be seen as project files that record things rather coarsely. All of them are still some distance from the goal.

Apart from waiting for progress in text models to bring us a cheap, efficient and usable synthetic data agent, there is a lot that can be done. To start the discussion, I list five of them here; each already has some work behind it, and I also try to describe what still separates that work from the goal.

### More realistic and more controllable virtual instruments

As mentioned earlier, the gap between synthetic and real data comes mainly from the quality of the virtual instruments, so we need to train a large number of more realistic ones. There is already a good deal of work here. DDSP makes traditional signal processing modules differentiable and trains them together with neural networks, so that pitch and loudness can be controlled independently [@ddsp]. MIDI-DDSP builds three levels on top of it, notes, performance and synthesis, aiming both for realistic sound and for detailed user control over the performance [@mididdsp]. torchsynth takes the purely synthetic direction: it is a modular synthesizer that runs on the GPU, released together with one billion sounds and their synthesis parameters [@torchsynth].

People have also trained instruments with methods borrowed from speech synthesis. Deep Performer borrows directly from TTS to synthesize performance audio from a score [@deepperformer]. Hawthorne et al. have a single model generate spectrograms from MIDI for arbitrary combinations of instruments; they trained one autoregressive and one diffusion version of the decoder, and diffusion was better [@spectrogramdiffusion]. TokenSynth uses a decoder-only transformer to generate codec audio tokens directly from MIDI tokens, with timbre specified by a CLAP embedding [@tokensynth]; MIDI-VALLE, adapted from VALL-E, turns both MIDI and audio into discrete tokens and focuses on piano [@midivalle]; FlowSynth uses flow matching together with inference-time search to keep an instrument's timbre consistent across pitches and velocities [@flowsynth].

Measured against the goal, what this work still lacks is mainly control signals and coverage. Its control signal is essentially MIDI, that is, pitch, duration and velocity; how the bow moves, how the player breathes and how each note is attacked are not in MIDI. The instruments covered are also still few: MIDI-VALLE does only piano, and FlowSynth notes that models generating one note at a time have difficulty keeping timbre consistent from note to note. Sample-based instruments, meanwhile, have the problem SynthSOD describes, where the same note is always the same handful of recorded samples over and over [@synthsod].

Perhaps the next things to push on are data collection and the design of control signals. For each instrument, we can start from the player's perspective, design the control signals it needs, and capture those signals together with the corresponding performance recordings. When data is insufficient, this can be combined with physical modeling, using large amounts of synthetic data for pretraining; or we can train on related instruments first and transfer at the end with a small amount of data from the target instrument. As for the model architecture itself, perhaps we can borrow the approach used in speech and take a small model pretrained on text data to predict the audio token sequence directly from the control token sequence. This could foster collaboration between technical researchers and music students, produce a large number of instruments that music producers can use day to day, and increase the diversity of the synthetic data that agents build.

### Making the agent's arranging, performing and mixing more human

Earlier I assumed that agents can arrange like a real person, which they can't do yet. There is relatively more work on mixing. Steinmetz et al. built a differentiable mixing console that can learn to mix multitracks from very few examples [@dmc]; Koo et al. trained an encoder that attends only to audio effects and can carry the mixing style of a reference song over to another set of multitracks [@mixingstyletransfer]. However, Martínez-Ramírez et al. also write that such models are still far from professional human mixes, one reason being the lack of dry recordings [@martinezramirez2022]. In performance, piano has data like MAESTRO, with precisely aligned audio and MIDI [@maestro], while other instruments have far less. In arrangement, systems like DAWZY can already let an agent edit the project itself [@dawzy], but I have yet to see work that measures whether its edits resemble those of a real arranger.

Perhaps the first thing to push on is measurement: how to measure the similarity of arranging, performing and mixing decisions. In mixing, the encoder from Koo et al. can count as a start; in arranging and performing, I haven't seen a counterpart. Once we can measure this, we can study another question: whether, by giving an agent a certain prompt and context (a reference, for example), we can have it imitate the decisions of different arrangers, performers and mixing engineers. As for data, perhaps we can begin by collecting project files of performances and arrangements, since the decisions are all recorded in the project files.

### Making rendering into infrastructure everyone can use

Some rendering tools already exist. DawDreamer can host plugins and render audio from Python [@dawdreamer]; WildFX puts REAPER in Docker [@wildfx]; torchsynth runs more than ten thousand times faster than real time on a single GPU [@torchsynth]. But what is fast in torchsynth is its own synthesizer; good-sounding commercial instruments cost money and are slow to render, and whether the rendered data can be shared publicly remains an open question.

In the short term, perhaps we can purchase commercial instruments to move this process forward while optimizing the infrastructure to make instrument rendering more efficient. As for how to organize this in the long term, perhaps we can draw on what other fields have done and set up organizations so that instrument rendering is carried out in a more distributed and fairer way. Speech has Common Voice, crowdsourced from more than fifty thousand people [@commonvoice], and images have the public LAION-5B [@laion5b]; music could certainly have non-profit organizations of its own that regularly share updated datasets through academic publications, so that academic labs and model providers can update their trainers accordingly and offer the corresponding control signals, allowing creators to control the models.

### Leave what text can't describe to inference time

Qualities like timbre and soundstage are hard to describe in language, even with the kind of data described above. For such qualities, we can study how to do a small amount of inference-time search and guidance at the rendering stage, within the given search space, so that music creators can adjust the model's output with a few samples. There is some related work already: DITTO optimizes the initial noise at inference time to control music generation [@ditto], SMITIN intervenes on MusicGen's attention heads at inference time [@smitin], and SCORE compares Best-of-N with evolutionary search on general text-to-audio, with the latter improving CLAP and PQ by 10.8% and 10.6% respectively [@score].

The search space of these methods is not yet constrained by fine-grained descriptions. Perhaps the next thing to push on is the objective of the search. I haven't seen work of this kind yet.

### Benchmarks that measure how far we are from the goal

Most existing benchmarks don't measure this goal directly. MuChoMusic tests audio language models' understanding of music with 1,187 multiple-choice questions and finds that the models rely too heavily on information from the language side [@muchomusic]. CMI-Bench recasts traditional MIR annotations as instructions and finds a large gap between audio language models and specially trained supervised models [@cmibench]. MusTBench tests temporal grounding [@mustbench]. On the generation side, CMI-RewardBench and SongEval test human preference [@cmirewardbench; @songeval]. None of them directly tests whether a description can recover the audio it describes.

Synthetic data may help here. Because the project file is known, we can have an understanding model describe the rendered audio and then compare its description with the project file, track by track and moment by moment; we can also change one thing in the description and see whether only that thing changes in the generation model's output. Understanding models and generation models can then be evaluated with the same set of data.

I think these problems are very well suited to academia. They contain many sub-problems, each needing a small loop: pose the problem, collect data, train a model, propose an evaluation. They don't take up much training compute, but they do require collaboration across disciplines and fields. Solving each small problem moves us toward the larger goal, and each can also be evaluated and used on its own.

## Finally

Recently a friend decided to cut off contact with me because I do AI research. She believes AI research is "mutilating people's souls." I obviously don't agree with her, but I can understand why she feels this way; I sometimes feel it myself. When vinyl records and the phonograph were first invented, the orchestras employed by theaters were dismissed in large numbers; but these technologies also gave rise to a new generation of artists, who explored sound effects and non-real-time overdubbing. More importantly, music and language spread, and far more people came to use them.

I believe my friends who make music wouldn't object to a better Logic Pro X; I also believe my friends who use Ableton can no longer put up with tape-based eight-track recorders. Suno and models like it are not a "better Logic Pro X" today, but they can be. What these fine-grained text descriptions should look like, and what information should go into them, is still something we can decide; together, we can put our own taste and spirit into them.

If we can do that, music models can respect artists' creative inspiration while letting more people take part, and letting everyone truly benefit from the process. We can also scale this system further and push it to its limit, until it becomes a new kind of tool for making music, a tool truly designed for artists and for artistic creation.

I hope to see this happen; judging from history, I believe it certainly will.
