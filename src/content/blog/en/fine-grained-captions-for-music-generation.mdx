I started making pop and applied music for the mainland Chinese market in 2017. I mostly worked as a composer, arranger and producer. My clients were in film, TV and games, and some were ordinary pop listeners. In 2021 I joined Professor Zhiyao Duan's lab at the University of Rochester. I started with busy work and slowly moved into deep learning. Now I lead research and labs at Smule, where a lean team and I are figuring out which models to train and how to train them. Since 2025 I've spent less and less time on music production. These days I mostly write demos for friends. I rarely do the arranging myself or take a song through full production.

I'm busy now, so I probably get less than an hour to focus on a song. The first half hour goes to melody, lyrics, chords and a rough groove. I write at the keyboard, and I sketch these out quickly with a piano or guitar sound. I'm much faster than a model at this step. Making the demo also helps me figure out what I want. Then I spend about ten minutes roughing in the other instruments I want and where they should go. I usually use Logic Pro X. I know it well, and its stock instruments are pretty good.

Then I turn to a music generation model like Suno. Mostly by editing the text description, I get it to produce the overall sound I want. The model helps me swap instruments and try different instrumentations. It also makes a demo I didn't sing very carefully sound better. In places where I haven't quite decided what I want, I have it try a few more versions until I find it. I usually pick a few versions, download all the stems, edit and remix them in Logic, and hand that in.

Unfortunately, I still can't hand in what these models produce as a finished arrangement and production. Compared with where language and video models are today, the gap is pretty big. In this post I want to make a guess about why. I think the gap is mainly there because music generation models need finer-grained text descriptions. To write such descriptions, a model first has to understand the music it hears. And we don't yet have the data to train that understanding.

## Models keep getting bigger and the metrics keep going up, but they don't sound better

I often hear two claims in the industry. One is that the capabilities of music generation models are easy to scale. The other is that they aren't.

If the goal is just to fit the pretraining data better, or to fit some set of human preferences better, there's no reason it shouldn't scale. And mostly it does. YuE compared three sizes: 0.5B, 2B and 7B. In human evaluation, the bigger the model and the training budget, the more its musicality was preferred [@yue]. MusicGen reported three sizes too: 300M, 1.5B and 3.3B. Perplexity went from 56.1 to 48.4 to 46.1, and FAD from 0.96 to 0.86 to 0.82. Both kept getting better [@musicgen]. Mikey Shulman, the CEO of Suno, said in a 2024 interview that their models scale about the way text models do: "bigger is usually better" [@shulman2024].

But for the same MusicGen models, the overall quality rated by humans (OVL) is 78.3, 81.9 and 79.2. It stops going up at 1.5B. The authors say so themselves: subjective quality doesn't improve past 1.5B, and the 3.3B model just seems to follow the text prompt more closely [@musicgen]. Speech has a similar finding. Cuervo and Marxer found that the linguistic ability of speech-only language models scales up to three orders of magnitude more slowly than that of text models [@cuervo2024]. Audio just asks for more from the data and from the supervision signal.

I think both claims are right. A bigger model does fit better. But going by the human ratings above, it doesn't necessarily sound better. Here are the three problems I run into most often. I think they hurt the usability of these models a lot, and I think they have the same solution: finer-grained text descriptions.

### The model only gets the gist of my text instructions

As I said, I mostly get what I want out of the model by editing the text description. The model should understand the instruction I give it, and use it to narrow down the space of outputs it searches. A good instruction pins the model down. In image and video, we can ask the output to meet pixel-level constraints: a luminance map, a depth map, normals from an untextured 3D model, or a specific character whose identity has to be kept. If I paste in a depth map, the model follows that depth map. If I ask for a fixed character, the model keeps that character.

In music generation, we often can't get control this direct. Some controls are direct, like melody, lyrics and singer identity. Some are more or less direct, like instruments and groove. And some are very vague and coarse, like style or the feel of a mood. To some degree, a creator's skill shows in how they turn vague controls into direct ones, one step at a time.

Research and products are both making control more concrete, little by little. Instruct-MusicGen lets the model add, remove or extract a stem when told to [@instructmusicgen]. Suno Studio can generate stems and export them as audio or MIDI [@sunostudio]. But once the instruction gets vague, the results drop fast. RIME has multimodal LLM agents do mixing and post-production from instructions. When the instruction spells out the effects and parameters, the edit similarity is 0.718 for GPT-Audio-Mini and 0.687 for Gemma 3n. When the instruction only describes the overall feel, the two drop to 0.519 and 0.528 [@rime].

Without this kind of fine-grained, direct control, I can't really control what a music generation model outputs.

### Instruments change while they're playing

The second problem is that instruments change by themselves in the output, and I hear it a lot. The most typical case is a brass section that blends into a string section with a similar role. Instruments that should stay on one melodic line jump around. The vocal comes and goes, or suddenly turns into another instrument. The drum sound and the soundstage of the song often jump too. Smaller jumps happen in almost every generation. I hear the size of the guitar, the recording conditions and the feel of the strings change all the time. If I ask for one brass instrument, I often get a continuous "spectrum of brass instruments." It slides from trumpet all the way to tuba. Sometimes it slides on to cello, and the cello may well slide on to double bass.

Papers report similar things. The SongGen authors note that when the mix is the direct training target, the model tends to model the more predictable accompaniment first and often neglects the vocal [@songgen]. A challenge paper trained on only 457 hours of data found that distinctive instruments like saxophone, harmonica and vibraphone often come out as generic timbres [@koh2026].

I suspect this has to do with how the model represents audio. A common practice now is to first compress audio into more "semantic" discrete tokens. That makes it easier for a language model to model them. The idea comes from speech research. MusicLM mostly keeps the structure of AudioLM: w2v-BERT produces the semantic tokens, and SoundStream produces the acoustic tokens [@musiclm].

In music, the line between semantic and acoustic isn't that clear. But people still train this way, and each group makes a different assumption about what counts as semantic. MERT uses an RVQ-VAE as the acoustic teacher and adds a CQT-based musical teacher [@mert]. MuQ predicts tokens produced by Mel-RVQ [@muq]. MuCodec first does masked language modeling, then adds mel-spectrogram and CQT reconstruction, plus a lyric transcription loss made of CTC and RNN-T [@mucodec]. The MuSeC authors say it more directly: these semantics-guided schemes were designed for speech and don't fit music well [@musec].

But what is "semantics" in music? I don't think this has ever had a clear definition. If we train with masked prediction, we're saying that the target we mask out and ask the model to predict is the semantics. Unfortunately, we often don't know if that target holds all the semantic information it should. If we ask the tokenizer to output text descriptions and lyrics from audio, then those descriptions and lyrics draw the boundary of the semantics.

My guess is that this is related to the jumps I hear. If the line itself isn't clear, the model may treat things that are "semantically" close, like trumpet and tuba or cello and double bass, as about the same thing and lump them together.

This approach is enough for speech and not enough for music. I think one reason is that the information in speech and in music looks different. Speech mostly has one speaker, and the identity is stable. The information basically changes along one direction, time. Music has both a horizontal and a vertical direction. Vertically, several parts are stacked at the same moment. The sources overlap heavily and are much harder to separate than in speech [@mousavi2025]. Horizontally, the role of each part keeps changing over time.

### Everything it generates sounds about the same

The third problem is mode collapse, and it's easy to see. No matter how I change the prompt, I get about the same thing. The model has too many opinions of its own. Simply put, RL has been overdone. Strangely, it also looks like RL hasn't been done enough. Control is still poor, yet the output is already very uniform. It's a bit like the collapse we saw in the GAN era.

A paper from August 2026 measured exactly this [@slendebroek2026]. The authors used 72 MIR features to analyze the output of Suno and Lyria 3 in four genres. They found that Lyria 3 has become less diverse within a genre, and that Suno flattens the differences between genres. Neither system follows the prompt faithfully. An ordinary classifier can tell AI tracks from human tracks almost perfectly.

There's already quite a bit of evidence that RLHF lowers the diversity of music. SMART tuned a symbolic music generation system with Audiobox Aesthetics as the reward. It found that over-optimization cuts output diversity sharply. After optimization, the output often scores higher on this reward than real performances do. The authors say this only shows the limits of using the reward as a proxy metric [@smart]. DRAGON found that optimizing per-song FAD makes Vendi diversity 17.7% worse on average [@dragon]. For language models, Kirk et al. also found that RLHF gives significantly less diverse output than SFT [@kirk2023].

Besides RL, classifier-free guidance also pushes diversity down, and it's used all the time at inference. On a MusicLM-based model, turning the guidance scale from 1 up to 7 drops the diversity score from 0.5 to 0.3 [@cideron2024cfg].

Part of RLHF's diversity problem comes from RL's own mode-seeking behavior. GX-Chen et al. go further. They point out that under common settings (weak regularization, or the same reward for every correct answer), the optimal target distribution of KL-regularized RL is often unimodal by itself. The collapse in diversity is basically written into the objective [@gxchen2025].

<details>
<summary>Derivation: why is the optimal distribution unimodal?</summary>

**Setup**: Call the model being trained $\pi$ and the reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (usually the model before RL starts). The reward of an output $y$ is $R(y)$, and the regularization strength is $\beta > 0$. KL-regularized RL (here I mean the most common kind, with reverse KL) maximizes

$$
J_\beta(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\big[R(y)\big] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big). \tag{1}
$$

**Optimal solution**: Expand the KL term by its definition, and move the reward inside the log:

$$
\begin{aligned}
J_\beta(\pi)
&= \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\Big[R(y) - \beta \log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}\Big] \\
&= -\beta\, \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\Big[\log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\, e^{R(y)/\beta}}\Big] \\
&= -\beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi \,\|\, G_\beta\big) + \beta \log Z_\beta ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
\begin{aligned}
G_\beta(y) &= \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\, e^{R(y)/\beta}}{Z_\beta}, \\
Z_\beta &= \sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y')\, e^{R(y')/\beta}.
\end{aligned} \tag{2}
$$

$Z_\beta$ doesn't depend on $\pi$. The KL divergence is never negative, and it is zero only when the two distributions are the same. So (1) has exactly one optimal solution: $\pi^* = G_\beta$. In other words, even with unlimited compute and perfect optimization, RL can only end up at $G_\beta$. It is the "target distribution" of this objective.

**Probability ratio**: Take any two outputs $y_1$ and $y_2$. The normalizing constant $Z_\beta$ cancels in the ratio, so

$$
\log \frac{G_\beta(y_1)}{G_\beta(y_2)} = \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2)} + \frac{R(y_1) - R(y_2)}{\beta}. \tag{3}
$$

The first term on the right is the preference the reference model already had. The second is the difference in reward divided by $\beta$. These two terms fully decide which output gets more probability in the target distribution.

**Case one**: all correct answers have the same reward. This is the standard setup for verifiable tasks: 1 for a right answer, 0 for a wrong one. For any two correct answers, the second term in (3) is zero, so

$$
\frac{G_\beta(y_1)}{G_\beta(y_2)} = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2)},
$$

and this ratio doesn't depend on $\beta$. RL only moves probability from wrong answers to right ones. The relative proportions among the right answers are copied from the reference model as they are. Whichever answer the reference model preferred, the target distribution prefers just as much, and making $\beta$ smaller doesn't help. If the reference model already puts very different probabilities on these answers, the target distribution is just as concentrated on one of them. Switching to forward-KL regularization doesn't change this.

**Case two**: the rewards differ and the regularization is weak. First look at two outputs with the same reference probability. Then (3) becomes

$$
\frac{G_\beta(y_1)}{G_\beta(y_2)} = \exp\!\Big(\frac{R(y_1) - R(y_2)}{\beta}\Big).
$$

A linear gap in reward becomes an exponential gap in probability. The paper gives an example: if the rewards differ by only 0.1 and $\beta$ is the commonly used $10^{-3}$, the ratio is $e^{100}$, more than $10^{43}$. The conclusion is the same when the reference probabilities differ. The first term in (3) is fixed, but the second keeps growing as $\beta$ gets smaller. When $\beta$ is small enough, whichever output has the slightly higher reward always takes the overwhelming majority.

**Condition for multiple modes**: For two outputs to have the same probability in the target distribution, the right side of (3) must be zero, that is,

$$
R(y_2) - R(y_1) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2)}. \tag{4}
$$

The difference in reward must exactly cancel the difference in log probability under the reference model. Here $\beta$ is just a knob. Turn it up, and you favor the outputs the reference model likes. Turn it down, and you favor the outputs with high reward. As long as two outputs have different rewards, there is at most one $\beta$ that makes them exactly even. Common settings usually can't meet this condition. So most of the probability in the target distribution sits on one mode, and isn't shared among all the high-quality outputs. The paper calls the shared case multimodal. The concentrated case is what I call unimodal here. This collapse doesn't come from too little exploration or from poor optimization. The better the optimization, the closer the model gets to $G_\beta$, and the more complete the collapse.

</details>

The other part comes from the reward model. We can't check what it has really learned or what it's rewarding. So the most common practice is to watch the RL run, grab some checkpoints and listen to them ourselves. Listening this way, it's hard to hear that diversity is lacking. You can only hear some problems in how the rewards are set up. And once a problem is found, the usual move is to add another reward, and then another.

Patching rewards like this can easily hurt generalization. The DiffRhythm 2 authors write that as the number of preferences grows, model merging often clearly lowers the average performance across them [@diffrhythm2]. TuneJury found that as the reward goes up, MAD, which measures distributional fidelity, gets worse. With expert iteration at a learning rate of 1e-5, the reward went up by 0.416 and MAD got worse by 0.669. The authors call this a classic sign of reward exploitation [@tunejury].

The deeper issue is that everyone has a different taste in music. MusicRL collected 300,000 pairs of user preferences. It found that text adherence and audio quality explain only part of them, and that fine-tuning on the quality reward alone easily over-optimizes [@musicrl]. CMI-RewardBench relabeled 500 test samples. Krippendorff's α between people was only about 0.50, and Gemini-2.5-Pro struggled to agree with human preferences more than 80% of the time [@cmirewardbench].

The aesthetic data and metrics we have all record some kind of consensus taste. SongEval asked 16 professional annotators with music backgrounds to rate 2,399 full songs on 5 dimensions [@songeval]. Audiobox Aesthetics splits aesthetics into four dimensions: production quality, production complexity, content enjoyment and content usefulness [@audioboxaesthetics]. Xiao et al. also show in theory that KL-regularized RLHF can, in extreme cases, lead to preference collapse, where minority preferences are almost completely ignored [@xiao2024].

This problem isn't fully solved even in text, where there's lots of data, lots of investment and plenty of research resources. People have just split the reward into finer and more objective pieces, and then put most of their training effort elsewhere.

## How does vision do it?

As I said at the start, music generation models are quite far behind video models today. So I want to look first at how vision does it. For a while now, the great majority of vision research teams have put most of their effort into one thing: finer-grained text descriptions.

At training time, they use a model to rewrite the descriptions of the training data. This ability to write descriptions can borrow directly from the prior of text models. DALL·E 3 trained a dedicated captioner to write descriptive synthetic captions, and in the end trained on 95% synthetic captions and 5% original ones. The paper also mentions that GPT-4 can "upsample" any short caption into a very detailed description [@dalle3]. PixArt-α relabeled LAION images with LLaVA. The number of nouns per caption went from 6.4 to 20.9, and the share of valid nouns from 8.5% to 13.3%. With SAM images labeled by LLaVA, the numbers are 29.3 and 18.6% [@pixartalpha]. SD3 swapped half of its training captions for ones generated by CogVLM. Its overall GenEval score went from 43.27 to 49.78. Position went from 6.50 to 18.00, and color attribution from 11.75 to 24.75 [@sd3].

It's the same in video. CogVideoX first has CogVLM write a dense caption for each frame. Then GPT-4 summarizes them into a caption for the whole video. Finally, the output of this pipeline is used to train an end-to-end video captioner [@cogvideox]. ShareGPT4Video has GPT-4V go frame by frame and describe what changed between neighboring frames. It labeled 40K videos this way, trained ShareCaptioner-Video on them, and then used that model to label 4.8M video clips [@sharegpt4video]. Newer models like SAM 3 can take a concept and detect, segment and track every matching object in every frame of a video [@sam3]. This information is also often used to synthesize more detailed captions.

At inference time, they use a text model to expand the user's short prompt into a detailed description. The Sora technical report is clear about it. It applies DALL·E 3's re-captioning to video, and it uses GPT to expand short user prompts into long captions before sending them to the video model [@sora]. Movie Gen uses LLaMA3 70B as a teacher and distills an 8B prompt rewriter from it [@moviegen]. HunyuanVideo also has a section just on prompt rewriting [@hunyuanvideo]. Prompt Expansion found that if you sample several expanded versions of the same prompt, you get images that are more diverse and also better looking [@promptexpansion].

Of course, denser captions aren't always better. Brack et al. ran controlled experiments and found that long, dense synthetic captions improve text alignment but can hurt aesthetics and sample diversity. Randomizing the caption length during training eases this trade-off [@brack2025].

So to a large degree, control over video generation models has become finer and more specific. It also depends more and more on the intelligence and understanding of language models.

Vision teams also use synthetic data very actively, and keep widening what the descriptions can cover. I'll come back to this point when I talk about music. Playing for Data rendered 25K images with GTA V. Game data plus one third of CamVid did slightly better than a model trained on all of CamVid [@playingfordata]. The teacher in Depth Anything V2 is trained entirely on synthetic images [@depthanythingv2]. CoSyn has an LLM write code to render 400K images and 2.7M rows of instruction data. On text-rich benchmarks, its average performance beats GPT-4V and Gemini 1.5 Flash [@cosyn].

## Music is doing fine-grained descriptions too, but they're not fine enough

Labeling training data with a model is already routine in music generation too. LeVo used Qwen2-Audio to give about 2 million songs open-vocabulary tags [@levo]. ACE-Step 1.5 first labeled 5 million samples with Gemini 2.5 Pro as a golden set. It then fine-tuned Qwen2.5-Omni into a dedicated captioner, kept improving it with a reward model and RL, and finally labeled the whole corpus of 27 million samples [@acestep15]. The captioners themselves are getting stronger too. Music Flamingo's MF-Skills data covers harmony, structure, timbre, lyrics and cultural context [@musicflamingo]. Qwen also released Qwen3-Omni Captioner on its own [@qwen3omnicaptioner].

People are working on the time dimension as well. FUTGA points out that existing music captioning methods can mostly only write a short overall description of a short clip, so it built fine-grained captions with timing information [@futga]. SegTune uses Audio Flamingo 3 to write a separate caption for each section. It then lays each section's prompt over the matching stretch of time and uses that as the condition for generation [@segtune].

But these understanding models still aren't capable enough. Ideally, a fine-grained description of a piece of music should be so detailed that, once all the text is written down, it maps back to one full piece of audio. A detailed enough caption can already do that for video. Today's models are still pretty far from this standard. MusTBench ran its tests in 2026 and found that existing audio language models struggle even with precise temporal grounding, especially with telling when a sound source ends. Music Flamingo's average score on temporal sound grounding (TSG) is only 38.5, and its score for hitting the end point within 3 seconds is only 24.0 [@mustbench].

Video understanding can already do this, so we can infer that the missing capability is mostly missing data. Fine-grained understanding data really is too expensive. MusicCaps has only 5.5k entries, all written by hand by professional musicians [@musiclm]. The LP-MusicCaps authors switched to having an LLM generate pseudo captions from tags for exactly this reason: collecting them by hand is "costly and time-consuming" [@lpmusiccaps].

## What if we suddenly had fine-grained understanding?

Let's suppose a perfect fine-grained description dataset suddenly fell from the sky. It has tens of millions of audio clips, and each description is detailed enough that you could almost recover the audio from the text. Clearly, music understanding would take off. We could use these understanding models right away to rewrite the descriptions in the training data of generation models, and generation models would get better at following instructions. The three problems I described above would all get better too.

First, the model would stop getting only the gist. Creators could really control it, and it would become a more faithful tool in their hands. If a description can recover the audio almost perfectly, then it really is a "project file." You can change it however you like. For audio-to-audio work, this understanding can also be used to decide what to keep, what to drop and how to change things.

Second, instruments wouldn't slide away so easily, because there would be less semantic ambiguity. Many tokenizers already use text as supervision. MuCodec adds a lyric recognition loss [@mucodec]. CosyVoice inserts vector quantization into the encoder of a speech recognition model to get supervised semantic tokens [@cosyvoice]. If a codec can produce a fine-grained text description, that at least shows the information has been kept. Fine-grained text supervision is exactly the kind of supervision signal it needs.

Representation learning has long had related results. Without any inductive bias on the model and the data, learning disentangled representations without supervision is impossible in theory [@locatello2019]. Models also pack more features than they have dimensions into the same set of dimensions [@superposition]. And there's an existing framework for measuring how well a representation separates information and how well it keeps it. It looks at disentanglement, completeness and informativeness separately [@dci].

This also helps the rendering that comes after. Flow matching and diffusion models aren't really good at handling this kind of ambiguity, and I don't think they should be the ones to handle it. The clearer the condition, the easier the step from semantic to acoustic, and the better the control. Seed-Music mentions this. Because lead sheet tokens don't describe acoustic features, they had to make the renderer that follows bigger to reach the same end-to-end quality [@seedmusic].

Third, the outputs would stop sounding the same. Once the caption narrows the search space, we can get diversity by changing the text. The reward also no longer has to be mixed up with poor instruction following. Kaleido Diffusion takes this approach for images. It first generates discrete latents such as text descriptions and bounding boxes autoregressively, and then conditions the diffusion on them. Richer conditions give more diverse outputs [@kaleido]. This way we can train just the model's ability to follow instructions, and that's easy to verify.

Finally, rewriting, expanding and reasoning over text can all be handed to existing LLMs. That means we can lean on language models. The better they get, the better music generation gets.

## This fine-grained description is really a project file

Stems, instrument recognition, key, meter, chords, transcription: in the end, these traditional MIR problems are all asking how to get a fine-grained caption. Of course such a dataset won't fall from the sky. I think it will most likely come from DAW project files. These labels used to be expensive. Now the cost is coming down, because GPT-6 Astra can already control a DAW (Digital Audio Workstation) directly, and can even generate DAW project files directly. In a project, we know what note each track plays at each frame, with what sound, and through what effects. So the labels are frame by frame, and almost free. Given the progress of the past few years, I believe quantization and distillation will only make the same level of intelligence cheaper.

This road already has some foundations. Slakh2100 rendered Lakh MIDI with professional sample-based instruments into 2100 multitrack mixes, 145 hours in total [@slakh2100]. WildFX puts REAPER in Docker and runs it headless. It supports VST, VST3, LV2 and CLAP plugins, and is used for blind estimation of mixing graphs and plugin parameters [@wildfx]. Systems that let an agent operate a DAW have started to show up too. DAWZY has GPT-5 generate Lua scripts that change a REAPER project through ReaPy. It also uses three MCP tools to query state, adjust parameters and generate beats [@dawzy]. The community has bridge tools like AbletonMCP as well [@abletonmcp].

Just like in vision, mixing synthetic data with real data works for music too. MuScriptor does multi-instrument transcription. Trained only on synthetic data, its onset F1 is 34.5. Fine-tuning on real data takes it to 54.4, and adding RL takes it to 60.4. The authors conclude that synthetic pretraining is very effective, especially when there's little real data, but that synthetic data alone isn't enough [@muscriptor]. For fingerstyle guitar transcription, purely procedural data gives an F1 of 66.23 and purely real data gives 81.42. Synthetic pretraining followed by real fine-tuning gives 83.49 [@murgul2025]. TAC builds synthetic mixtures out of real audio material and uses them to train a timestamped audio captioner. It reaches state of the art on TACOS, and the authors admit there's still a sim-to-real gap [@tac].

We have to be honest about this gap between synthetic and real. It mostly comes from the quality of the virtual instruments. SynthSOD trained separation models on synthetic orchestral data. On real recordings, none of the models separated well, not even on the fairly simple URMP. The authors think the main reason is that sample-based instruments cycle through a few recorded samples in round-robin fashion, so the real diversity of the data is very limited [@synthsod].

Suppose we suddenly had very high-quality virtual instruments. Then we could have agents generate synthetic data in bulk. We know every detail in the project file, so the project itself gives us very fine-grained understanding data. Now suppose agents can also arrange like a real person[^agent]. Then we'd have all the data we need.

[^agent]: They'll get there. Don't underestimate what agents can do.

With the data, we could first train small classifiers one by one (stems, instrument activity detection, key, meter), and then put their outputs together into a full caption. One step further is a transcriber that can transcribe every instrument and recover the whole project directly.

Today we're still far from that. In the 2025 multi-instrument transcription challenge, eight teams submitted valid results and only two beat the MT3 baseline [@amtchallenge2025]. Systems like YourMT3+ output notes sorted by instrument, which means pitch and timing. They don't yet give timbre, effects or mixing parameters [@yourmt3plus].

## Using an open DAW project format as the intermediate representation

Project files can be used to build understanding data. I think they can also be used directly as the condition for a generation model. A lot of work already guides generation with some kind of intermediate condition. Music ControlNet controls generation with melody, dynamics and rhythm that change over time [@musiccontrolnet]. JASCO combines symbolic conditions like chords and melody with audio conditions to make generation controllable in time [@jasco]. MuseControlLite supports several kinds of control with lightweight conditioners [@musecontrollite]. Seed-Music uses lead sheets as its intermediate representation [@seedmusic].

Going a step further, a lot of recent work first generates a symbolic intermediate representation and then generates the audio. YuE2 first writes an ABC score [@yue2]. StepAudio 3 Music plans the arrangement in ABC notation (ABC-CoT) [@stepaudio3music]. Qwen-Music plans with melody tokens [@qwenmusic].

I think these are all good intermediate steps, but we can go many steps further. If there were an open DAW project format, it would be a better intermediate representation than MIDI or ABC. MIDI basically only handles notes, and ABC records even less. A project file has tracks, clips, notes and expression, automation, plugins and plugin states. Almost every decision the musician made is recorded in it. If we turn it into text and use that as the condition, it's very close to letting a musician work the DAW directly. At that point the generation model is mainly in charge of rendering. The search space is tightly constrained, the distribution it has to learn is more certain, and details like instrument timbre are easier to control.

A format like this already exists. DAWproject, from Bitwig and PreSonus, can record the audio, notes, note expression and automation data on the timeline. Plugin states are embedded as preset files [@dawproject]. Plugin states use each plugin's own format. But if every state is turned into a detailed enough description in language, that's no longer a problem.

## A shared goal

At this point we can see what the overall goal of AI music as a research field could be. We should build, as soon as possible, a large amount of paired text-music data, fine-grained enough that the audio can almost be recovered from the text. And the most feasible technical route right now is to have LLM-driven agents produce synthetic data at scale.

A lot of the work I mentioned can be seen in light of this goal. Captioners, temporal grounding and transcription go from audio toward description. Slakh2100 and agents that operate DAWs go from project toward audio. Lead sheets and ABC can be seen as project files that record things more coarsely. All of them are still some distance from the goal.

We could wait for progress in text models to bring us a cheap, efficient and usable synthetic data agent. But there's a lot we can do besides waiting. To get the discussion going, I'll write down five things here. Each one already has some work behind it, and I also try to write down what's still missing between that work and the goal.

### More realistic and more controllable virtual instruments

As I said, the gap between synthetic and real data mostly comes from the quality of the virtual instruments. So we need to train a lot of more realistic ones. There's already a good amount of work here. DDSP makes traditional signal processing modules differentiable and trains them together with neural networks. Pitch and loudness can be controlled separately [@ddsp]. MIDI-DDSP builds three levels on top of it: notes, performance and synthesis. It wants the sound to be realistic, and it also wants users to have detailed control over the performance [@mididdsp]. torchsynth goes for pure synthesis. It's a modular synthesizer that runs on the GPU, and it released a billion sounds with their synthesis parameters [@torchsynth].

People have also trained instruments with methods borrowed from speech synthesis. Deep Performer borrows directly from TTS and synthesizes performance audio from a score [@deepperformer]. Hawthorne et al. have one model generate spectrograms from MIDI for any combination of instruments. They trained the decoder twice, once autoregressively and once with diffusion, and diffusion was better [@spectrogramdiffusion]. TokenSynth uses a decoder-only transformer to generate codec audio tokens directly from MIDI tokens, and specifies the timbre with a CLAP embedding [@tokensynth]. MIDI-VALLE is adapted from VALL-E. It turns both MIDI and audio into discrete tokens and only does piano [@midivalle]. FlowSynth uses flow matching plus search at inference time to keep one instrument's timbre consistent across pitches and velocities [@flowsynth].

Compared with the goal, what this work still lacks is mostly control signals and coverage. The control signal is basically MIDI, which means pitch, duration and velocity. MIDI doesn't tell you how the bow moves, how the player breathes, or how each note starts. The instruments covered are still few. MIDI-VALLE only does piano, and FlowSynth notes that models that generate note by note have a hard time keeping the timbre consistent from one note to the next. Sample-based instruments have the problem SynthSOD describes. The same note is always the same few recorded samples [@synthsod].

Maybe what we can push forward next is data collection, and the design of control signals. For each instrument, we can start from the player's point of view and design the control signals it needs. Then we record those signals together with the matching performance. When there isn't enough data, we can combine this with physical modeling and pretrain on lots of synthetic data. We can also train on a similar instrument first and then transfer with a small amount of data from the target instrument. As for the model architecture, maybe we can borrow from speech: take a small model pretrained on text, and have it predict the audio token sequence directly from the control token sequence. This could bring technical researchers and people learning music together. It could produce a lot of instruments that music producers can use day to day, and it would make the synthetic data built by agents more diverse.

### Making the agent's arranging, performing and mixing more human

Above I assumed agents can arrange like a real person. Today they can't. There's relatively more work on mixing. Steinmetz et al. built a differentiable mixing console that can learn to mix multitracks from very few examples [@dmc]. Koo et al. trained an encoder that only cares about audio effects, and it can move the mixing style of a reference song onto another set of multitracks [@mixingstyletransfer]. But Martínez-Ramírez et al. also write that models like these are still far from professional human mixes, and one reason is the lack of dry recordings [@martinezramirez2022]. For performance, piano has data like MAESTRO, where audio and MIDI are precisely aligned [@maestro]. Other instruments have much less. For arrangement, systems like DAWZY can already let an agent edit the project itself [@dawzy]. But I haven't seen work that measures whether its edits look like a real arranger's.

Maybe what we can push first is a way to measure, that is, how to measure how similar arranging, performing and mixing decisions are. For mixing, the encoder from Koo et al. can count as a start. For arranging and performing I haven't seen anything like it. Once we can measure, we can study another question: if we give an agent some prompt and context (a reference, for example), can it imitate the decisions of different arrangers, performers and mixing engineers? As for data, maybe we can start by collecting project files of performances and arrangements, because the decisions are all recorded in the project files.

### Making rendering into infrastructure everyone can use

There are already some tools for rendering. DawDreamer can host plugins and render audio in Python [@dawdreamer]. WildFX put REAPER in Docker [@wildfx]. torchsynth runs more than ten thousand times faster than real time on a single GPU [@torchsynth]. But what's fast in torchsynth is its own synthesizer. Good-sounding commercial instruments cost money and are slow to render, and whether the rendered data can be shared publicly is still an open question.

In the short term, maybe we can buy commercial instruments to move this along, and at the same time improve the infrastructure so that rendering is more efficient. As for how to organize this in the long term, maybe we can learn from other fields and start organizations, so that rendering happens in a more distributed and fairer way. Speech has Common Voice, crowdsourced by more than fifty thousand people [@commonvoice]. Images have the public LAION-5B [@laion5b]. Music could certainly have some non-profits too. They could share updated datasets regularly by publishing academic papers. Academic labs and model providers could then update their trainers to match and offer the corresponding control signals, so that creators can control the models.

### Leave what text can't describe to inference time

Qualities like timbre and soundstage are hard to describe in words. That's true even with the kind of data I described above. For these qualities, we can study how to do a small amount of search and guidance at inference time, during rendering and within the given search space. Then music creators could adjust what the model generates with a few samples. There's some related work already. DITTO optimizes the initial noise at inference time to control music generation [@ditto]. SMITIN intervenes on MusicGen's attention heads at inference time [@smitin]. SCORE compares Best-of-N and evolutionary search on general text-to-audio, and the latter improves CLAP and PQ by 10.8% and 10.6% [@score].

For now, the search space of these methods isn't constrained by fine-grained descriptions. Maybe what we can push forward next is the objective of the search. I haven't seen work like that yet.

### Benchmarks that measure how far we are from the goal

Most existing benchmarks don't measure this goal directly. MuChoMusic uses 1,187 multiple-choice questions to test how well audio language models understand music, and finds that the models rely too much on the language side [@muchomusic]. CMI-Bench rewrites traditional MIR annotations as instructions, and finds a big gap between audio language models and supervised models trained for the task [@cmibench]. MusTBench tests temporal grounding [@mustbench]. On the generation side, CMI-RewardBench and SongEval test human preferences [@cmirewardbench; @songeval]. None of them directly tests whether a description can recover the audio it describes.

Maybe synthetic data can help here too. Because the project file is known, we can ask an understanding model to describe the rendered audio, and then compare its description with the project file, track by track and moment by moment. We can also change just one spot in the description and see if only that spot changes in the generation model's output. This way, understanding models and generation models can be evaluated with the same data.

I think these problems are a great fit for academia. There are lots of sub-problems under them, and each one needs a small loop: pose the problem, collect data, train a model, propose an evaluation. They don't take much training compute, but they do need people from different disciplines and fields to work together. Solving each small problem moves us toward the big goal at the end. Each one can also be evaluated and used by itself.

## Finally

Recently, a friend decided to stop being friends with me because I do AI research. She thinks AI research is "mutilating people's souls." Obviously I don't agree with her. But I can understand why she feels that way. Sometimes I feel it too. When vinyl records and the phonograph were first invented, the orchestras that theaters employed were let go in large numbers. But these technologies also gave rise to a new generation of artists. They explored sound effects, and they explored overdubbing that wasn't done in real time. More importantly, music and language spread, and many more people got to use them.

I believe my friends who make music wouldn't mind a better Logic Pro X. I also believe my friends who use Ableton couldn't stand a tape-based eight-track recorder anymore. Suno and models like it aren't a "better Logic Pro X" today, but they can be. What this fine-grained text description should look like, and what information should go into it, is still up to us. We can each put our own taste and spirit into it, together.

If we can do this, music models can respect the creative inspiration of artists and still let more people join in. Everyone can really benefit from the process. We can also scale this system bigger and push it further, until it becomes a new kind of tool for making music, a tool designed for artists and for making art.

I hope to see this happen. Looking at history, I believe it will.
